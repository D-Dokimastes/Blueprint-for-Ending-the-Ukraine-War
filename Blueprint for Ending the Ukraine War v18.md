## **Executive Summary** 

A durable end to the war is unlikely to emerge from moral assertion, maximal territorial rhetoric, or additional layers of declaratory guarantees from outsiders. 

It requires an architecture in which Ukraine survives as a united state; Russia can sign because defined security and community-protection objectives are addressed; Crimea and Sevastopol cease to function as permanent triggers for renewed war; and external actors can verify and support the arrangement without replacing the principals. 

This blueprint leverages constitutional changes to create a possible starting point for negotiations. Ukraine becomes a united, strong and neutral state modelled on Switzerland, in which no community may dominate or be dominated by another. This addresses stated Russian concerns regarding the Russian-speaking east and ensures that Ukraine cannot serve as a foreign (NATO) military platform against Russia. The Ukraine–Russia land border returns to its pre-2014 alignment - confirmed by referendum on the Ukrainian constitution both in the disputed eastern regions and nationally. Crimea converts to a special constitutional status of Ukrainian–Russian co-sovereignty: demilitarised, largely self-governing in ordinary affairs, and protected against domination by any community. Sevastopol becomes a completely civilian free port through a phased twenty-five-year fleet transition, ending its use as a military platform by any state. Russia may also require some constitutional adjustments to accommodate the agreed settlement following local referendums in Eastern Ukraine and Crimea. Ordinary Russians receive accelerated elimination of restrictions, followed by lifting of other sanctions on Russia as implementation proceeds.  Initial consideration of the negotiating text should occur through confidential, non-attributable channels if possible. 

The objective is not to write a treaty for Ukraine and Russia. It is to define the problem better, separate it into negotiable modules, and create a process in which the principals can produce an agreement they own. The final agreement should avoid leaving any territorial and security alignment issues unresolved thus preventing either Ukraine or Russia from using these as future renewed _casus belli_ . 

_Core test: Peace must become less dangerous, less costly, and more politically survivable than continued war._ 

## **1. Origins of the Conflict: Overlapping “Imagined Communities”** 

The war is not reducible to a single cause. Territory, military alignment, language, historical memory, regime interest, and security fears are connected expressions of a deeper collision between overlapping imagined communities. 

### **Ukraine** 

Ukraine increasingly understands itself as a sovereign nation-state capable of solving internal disagreements without foreign meddling: entitled to fixed geographic borders, an independent political system, its own alliances, language balance, and historical narrative. For Ukraine, loss of territory is experienced as national disappearance. This claim need not be accepted to be treated as a live political constraint. 

### **Russia** 

Russian political imagination tends to treat Russian-speaking parts of eastern and southern Ukraine as belonging to a wider Russian historical and cultural community. This claim need not be accepted to be treated as a live political constraint. It helps explain why Russian actors frame Ukrainian separation,

### **Europe and the United States**

## **2. First Movements Towards a Negotiable Reality Frame** 

A leaked Kremlin presentation (After Victory) from May 7<sup>th</sup> states: _« Перебор - это поражение, продолжение СВО стало бы пирровой победой.» (“Going too far spells defeat; continuing the Special Military Operation could turn victory into a Pyrrhic one.”)_<sup>_1_</sup> 

Ukraine's 4 June 2026 open letter to Russia also contains a narrow but important movement toward an operational frame. It states that: “The front line today is the line from which diplomacy must begin.” 

This does not concede sovereignty, formally recognize territorial  arrangements, or abandon claims for justice. It acknowledges that negotiation must begin from the actual distribution of military control rather than from an undefined final standard of justice. It is a shift towards realism on both sides, albeit shrouded in hostile rhetoric for a domestic audience. 

Justice may remain a moral objective. It is not, by itself, an executable settlement mechanism. Competing parties can each seek justice while treating the other's survival as intolerable; that is unlikely to produce peace. A settlement process cannot begin by resolving ultimate historical or moral claims that have remained contested for centuries. It must begin by identifying present constraints. 

- The line of military control. 

- The security concerns of each regime. 

- The populations and territories at issue. 

- The domestic political limits on concession. 

- The guarantees required to make compliance safer than renewed war. 

_The front line is not the final border. It is a fact from which a settlement process must start._ 

## **3. The Realpolitik Problem** 

The immediate objective is not reconciliation, moral agreement, or historical consensus. It is a settlement that both regimes can sign and survive. 

Acceptable to both parties does not mean both sides regard the outcome as just or desirable. It means neither side experiences signing as regime-threatening surrender, national self-erasure, or strategic suicide.

## **4. War Continuation Risk** 

Continuation of the war may itself become incompatible with the long-term survival of one or both regimes. The costs are cumulative on both sides, although the mechanisms differ. 

### **Ukraine** 

- Demographic depletion and emigration. 

- Destruction and deferred reconstruction. 

- Fiscal dependence on external support. 

- Mobilisation fatigue and political-legitimacy strain. 

- Exposure to changes in U.S. and European support. 

- Durable loss of economic and human capacity. 

Ukraine may remain capable of resistance while becoming progressively less capable of restoring a normal, self-sustaining state. 

### **Russia** 

- Military casualties and mobilisation pressure. 

- Economic distortion, sanctions, and technological constraint. 

- Growing dependence on China and external military suppliers. 

- Elite fatigue, distributional conflict, and internal security strain. 

- The political cost of an open-ended war without a credible endpoint. 

- Increasing exposure of Russian territory to long-range attack. 

Russia may remain capable of continuing the war while becoming progressively less able to carry its domestic and geopolitical costs. 

### **Decision threshold** 

No observer can reliably assign a precise probability to regime failure. The important shift is perceptual. A low, tolerable risk of systemic failure can coexist with continued war if leaders believe victory or improved leverage remains plausible. When perceived continuation risk rises materially over a multi-year horizon, the calculation can reverse: a settlement previously treated as humiliating may become the less dangerous option. 

This does not guarantee peace. Rising risk can produce settlement-seeking or riskier escalation. The difference is whether leaders believe a deal preserves the system they are trying to protect. 

_The relevant choice may increasingly be between a controlled settlement and uncontrolled political, economic, or military failure._ 

## **5. General Solution: The Swiss Confederation Model After the Sonderbund War** 

The general blueprint is the Swiss Confederation's post-Sonderbund settlement. After internal war, Switzerland did not solve its problem by partition, permanent domination by the victorious side, or reliance on an outside guarantor. It created a stronger federal state while retaining substantial cantonal autonomy, local identity, and institutional protection against unilateral centralisation. 

_The Swiss analogy supplies a constitutional method, not a claim that Ukraine and nineteenthcentury Switzerland share the same historical conditions, nor is the scale of the conflict in any way comparable._ 

The transferable principle is that decisions should be taken at the lowest competent level, avoiding unnecessary gridlock from regional disagreement. Ordinary federal government must remain

### **A. Constitutional allocation of power** 

- One sovereign Ukrainian state: national borders, defence, specialized national law enforcement, currency, treaty capacity, federal infrastructure, and foreign representation remain federal executive functions; constitutionally protected municipalities (hromadas) as the primary units of local selfgovernment, culture, language, education, and ordinary civil administration; rayons as canton-like territorial representatives and voluntary service-pooling bodies; and no separate oblast tier of government. 

- All present oblast functions shall be reassigned to the national government, rayons, municipalities, autonomous institutions or the private sector according to subsidiarity<sup>2</sup> , after which the oblast tier shall be dismantled and abolished. 

- Bicameral national parliament: Modelled on the Swiss Federal Assembly; a lower chamber representing the population of all Ukraine by number of votes gained until chamber filled and an upper chamber representing the rayons as constituent territorial units, with one Senator elected from each rayon by first-past-the-post. 

- All elective mandates shall be filled through elections contested by individual candidates rather than through party lists. This (Post-Sonderbund model) is intended to reduce institutional incentives for politics organized primarily around ethnic, linguistic or regional party blocs. 

- Effective mechanisms ensuring post-election integrity and continued democratic accountability, such as recall procedures and/or other arrangements<sup>3</sup> enabling voters to withdraw or reassign political representation without waiting for the next general election. 

- Civic coexistence: Citizenship does not require cultural uniformity. 

- Local democratic decision-making: Design must aim to suppress risk of local conflict flare-up by mechanisms to protect local minorities, likely requiring broad support in decision-making. 

- No right of secession, no separate local foreign policy, and no local right to invite foreign forces. 

- No designated official language, but distinct languages and cultures are protected as part of Ukraine's historical and civic inheritance and may not be restricted. (Swiss model). 

- The Constitution shall be maintained in Ukrainian and Russian as equally authentic texts. An English reference version may also be maintained for international purposes. Constitutional interpretation shall be strictly limited to meanings common to both authentic texts. 

- Language access: citizens may receive public services in Ukrainian or Russian where reasonably required. Municipalities may establish broader bilingual administration through their own constitutional powers.

### **B. Rights and exercise of public power** 

- Citizenship: Clear commonly acceptable criteria for recognition of citizenship, multiple citizenships must be permissible, voluntary renunciation of Ukrainian citizenship to allow for conversion to permanent residency status - if desired. 

- Institutional Neutrality: Public institutions must serve all citizens equally. In the exercise of public power, no public authority, public institution, or holder of public office may favour or disadvantage any citizen or community on the basis of ethnicity, language, religion, sex, sexual orientation, expressed opinions, cultural identity, or other comparable personal or civic characteristic. Ultimately, every person possesses equal inherent dignity. All public authority shall respect and protect that dignity. 

- Bounded Public Power **:** Every exercise of public authority must have a lawful basis, pursue a legitimate public purpose, and be necessary and proportionate to that purpose. 

- Personal Responsibility for Public Power: Every holder of public office bears personal responsibility for the lawfulness of their official acts. A public officer who reasonably doubts the lawfulness or constitutionality of an instruction shall raise the objection through the prescribed chain of review. No instruction shall excuse conduct that manifestly violates human dignity, constitutional rights, or criminal law. 

- Duty to Refuse Manifestly Unlawful Orders: No member of the police, armed forces, security services, or public administration shall execute an order whose unlawfulness is manifest, including an order requiring criminal conduct, collective punishment, or violation of human dignity. Personal liability for damages may ensue in case of breach. 

- Treason: Knowing and intentional violation or circumvention, whether by act or deliberate omission, of a constitutionally protected provision by any person exercising public authority shall constitute treason. No other act shall constitute treason. Guilt shall be determined by a vote of more than two-thirds of a jury of twelve selected by sortition from among eligible citizens, excluding active public servants. Upon conviction, the person shall be permanently disqualified from holding public office, holding public employment, and providing goods or services to the public sector, including entities with public participation. Additional penalties may apply as specified by law. Appeal shall be available according to normal procedure; where an appeal requires reconsideration of guilt, guilt shall be determined by a newly selected jury of twelve citizens under these same rules. No official immunity or privilege shall bar investigation or prosecution for treason. Investigation and prosecution shall proceed, according to the normal evidentiary thresholds applicable to serious criminal offences. This does not restrict advocacy, political activity, or any lawful procedure seeking amendment or repeal of any provision of the Constitution. This provision shall itself be included among the initial constitutionally protected provisions. 

- Generational constitutional referendum to maintain legitimacy: Consider requiring that constitutional provisions obtain affirmative support, once per generation (every 25 years), from more than 50% of the entire electorate to retain constitutional status. This would tend to preserve at constitutional level only provisions enjoying broad and continuing support, while returning outdated and more divisive questions to ordinary democratic politics and law. Fundamental individual rights, minority rights, constitutionally reserved municipal rights, and reciprocal international obligations merit enhanced protection, but no provision need be placed permanently beyond reconsideration. For these protected provisions, failure to secure the required support of more than 50% results in downgrading to ordinary status, thus providing a route to removal in two consecutive generational referendums. Conversely, any ordinary provision that obtains at least 2/3 support amongst the electorate is upgraded to protected status. Any constitutional amendments should remain provisional until the next generational review. Approval of an amendment in the generational referendum then removes its provisional status, while failure of an amendment or an ordinary constitutional provision restores the constitutional position preceding its adoption. - Defence Organization: Ukraine’s defense shall be organized primarily around a broadly trained citizen reserve and territorially distributed mobilisation capacity, supported by a limited professional standing force sufficient for continuous readiness, command, training, technical operations, and defence of the state.

### **Ukrainian constitutional review process** 

The constitutional provisions suggested in this blueprint are intended to serve as a minimal reference basis for a comprehensive Ukrainian constitutional review. The purpose is not merely to facilitate the final peace settlement by ensuring protections afforded to Russian-speaking life in the east, but to strengthen the constitutional order, improve the relationship between the state and its citizens, reinforce the rule of law, reduce corruption, and provide durable safeguards for peaceful democratic coexistence within a diverse society across Ukraine. The detailed constitutional design remains a matter for the Ukrainian constitutional process, however the resulting constitutional text must adequately address the specific issues identified in the Final Peace Agreement. 

### **Reciprocal constitutional implementation** 

The proposed settlement is not a unilateral constitutional concession by Ukraine. A durable package involving armed neutrality, Crimea, Sevastopol, and territorial reintegration presupposes constitutional and domestic legal implementation in both Ukraine and Russia, in the forms required by their respective constitutional orders. 

For legitimacy, the Ukrainian constitutional settlement should be submitted to an all-Ukrainian referendum after a defined period of post-ceasefire implementation, provisionally three to five years. The delay would permit demobilisation, civilian return, institutional stabilisation and visible experience of peace before the electorate is asked to confirm the permanent constitutional order. The referendum should require both a national majority and a combined majority across Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson and Kharkiv oblasts, voting together as one eastern constitutional approval block. These oblasts may remain in transitional operation until the referendum and subsequent constitutional implementation are complete. This arrangement gives no foreign state authority over Ukraine’s constitutional process. It ensures that the permanent settlement is accepted both nationally and by the population of the territories most directly affected by occupation, military contestation and reintegration, rather than being imposed by one domestic bloc upon another. Approval by both the national electorate and the combined eastern constitutional approval block shall confirm the permanent Ukrainian constitutional settlement and the reintegration of the affected oblasts. Upon certification of that result, Russia shall complete the agreed constitutional and legal accommodation and the verifiable transition of its forces from those territories in accordance with the Final Peace Agreement. 

The referendum converts eastern consent into a shared success condition: Kyiv must earn it, Moscow must support it, and both bear heavy costs if it fails. Successful reintegration of affected eastern populations to Ukrainian political life under a new constitution will help make the neutrality settlement durable from within. Russia’s incentive therefore shifts from maintaining territorial control to encouraging constitutional approval by the populations whose long-term security concerns the settlement is intended to address. 

For Russia and Ukraine, a Crimean co-principality would also require an explicit constitutional and legal accommodation rather than an attempt to conceal shared sovereignty beneath unilateral terminology. Before the Crimean ratification referendum, both states should adopt binding enabling instruments committing them to complete the required constitutional and legal accommodation upon satisfaction of defined local ratification and implementation milestones. These changes should be treated as reciprocal final-stage conditions of a package whose security, territorial, economic, and enforcement elements are agreed together. ### **NATO posture adjustment declaration (Conditional on Ukrainian Constitutional changes)** 

Permanent closure of Ukraine’s NATO accession track; no NATO bases, forces, nuclear weapons or long-range strike systems in Ukraine; termination of Ukraine-specific military integration; reciprocal NATO–Russia force, missile and exercise restraints in agreed border regions, including Ukraine; restored military consultation and verification; and a staged normalization of relations linked to implementation of the Final Peace Agreement. 

### **European Strategic Theatre Military Risk Reduction and Transparency Initiative** 

NATO and Russia commit to pursue additional reciprocal confidence-building measures designed to reduce military friction, prevent misunderstandings and improve strategic stability in Europe, including enhanced transparency, reciprocal enhanced observation of major military exercises close to borders, advance notification arrangements, military-to-military communication mechanisms, incidentprevention protocols, and other mutually agreed confidence-building measures. 

### **European Union association without military bloc incorporation** 

Ukraine may pursue deep European economic association, including EFTA/EEA-like market access, Schengen-style mobility, reconstruction integration, regulatory convergence, and selected defenceindustrial cooperation. ### **The Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund** 

The existing United States–Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund should be expanded as part of the Settlement to admit Russia alongside the United States as an external limited partner. Both receive the same preferential investment, bidding, matching, offtake and other rights. Resources currently under Russian control pass to Ukrainian control and enter the Fund’s opportunity pool. 

Ukraine recovers resource control and gains access to American and Russian capital, technology and mining capability. The United States retains its existing investment structure while gaining early option to start exploration and mining in Ukraine, including eastern resources presently under Russian control. Russia exchanges exclusive control over those resources for preferential access to the larger Ukrainian resource opportunity. Peace itself increases the value of the enlarged pool by making presently inaccessible or high-risk resources investable. Furthermore, in terms of their core technical strengths, U.S. and Russian mining and metallurgical expertise are strongly complementary. This makes Russia’s admission to the Fund into a positive-sum exchange for all. Thus American and Russian investments and supply interests would coexist within the same sovereign Ukrainian framework. Renewed war would threaten the interests of both. This converts what might otherwise become a predominantly American strategic stake in restored territory into shared economic exposure to the Settlement’s success. 

The alternatives force harder rejection points. Leaving the bilateral arrangement unchanged would cause resources relinquished by Russia to enter a preferential U.S.–Ukraine structure, adding an economic and strategic cost to Russian acceptance and withdrawal. Ukraine possesses critical-mineral deposits, reported to include rare earths (a geopolitical wild card), although their commercially recoverable scale in peacetime remains uncertain. If significant deposits contribute to alternative supply chains for materials presently concentrated elsewhere, particularly China, their strategic value could substantially exceed their mining value. 

Moscow could consequently perceive major American mining, processing or supply-chain investments as creating an implicit U.S. security interest: a strategic stake increasing the probability of American involvement in a future conflict. In the strongest interpretation, resources relinquished through Russian withdrawal could help create a US de facto security guarantee to Ukraine with practical effects perceived as indistinguishable from article 5. 

The visible alternative solution to this conundrum would require dissolving the existing United States– Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund, an agreement already concluded and publicly presented as a strategic partnership. Equivalent Russian participation in the Fund avoids both problems while enlarging the resource opportunity available through the Fund and making the opportunities available presently, not years from now or never. 

Russian admission and inclusion of resources in restored territories should occur reciprocally as part of the territorial settlement. Neither external partner should be able unilaterally to alter fundamental governance or preferential rights; ordinary commercial decisions should remain commercial. Existing lawful investments, accrued rights and obligations should be preserved or converted without uncompensated impairment except where otherwise agreed. The Fund’s governance structure should adopt the joint-governance principles described later for the North Crimean Canal, adjusted for the differences in objectives. Crimea is excluded from this structure.

## **6. Crimea and Sevastopol: A Special Solution**<sup>**4**</sup> 

Crimea cannot be treated as an ordinary territorial issue appended to a ceasefire line. It combines competing sovereignty claims, naval geography, Russian historical symbolism, Ukrainian legal claims, Crimean Tatar rights of return, population displacement, military infrastructure, and Black Sea security. 

Crimea’s population already endorsed the restoration of autonomous status in a 1991 referendum, before the constitutional order surrounding the peninsula had fully settled. The subsequent dissolution of the Soviet Union and the unresolved question of autonomy’s scope produced prolonged constitutional dispute rather than a durable local settlement. 

This proposal does not claim to replicate the 1991 arrangement. It seeks to build on its underlying principle: that Crimea requires a constitutionally protected autonomous order, now designed in a form capable of being accepted by Ukraine, Russia, the Crimean Tatars, and the peninsula’s other communities. 

In this context, the type of structure more likely to be negotiable is one in which neither side exercises exclusive sovereign authority or military control over Crimea, and both accept costly and permanent limits. 

### **Primary option: Andorra-inspired co-principality** 

The primary option is a Crimean co-principality: functionally, a limited condominium in which Ukraine and Russia share sovereignty, Crimea governs ordinary life locally, and the co-principals retain only a closed set of constitutional and security-relevant powers. The analogy concerns shared headship and local self-government, not a claim that Crimea can reproduce Andorra’s history or institutional form. Alternative options may be considered; an Åland-type autonomy layer; a Svalbardtype treaty layer; or a Panama-style transition, each with a distinct trade-off matrix. 

_Core bargain: Russia gives up exclusive possession. Ukraine gives up exclusive restoration. Neither side may militarise or absorb Crimea unilaterally._

For the purpose of the Crimean ratification referendum, the proposed final Crimean constitution’s provisions on voter eligibility, voting procedure, secrecy, verification, and protection of each community would operate provisionally for its own ratification. ## **8. Roadmap to an Executable Agreement Package** 

The final agreement must be constructed by the principals themselves. The purpose of this architecture is not to impose a finished treaty, but to provide a single negotiating text: a complete starting shape of the bargain, so that the hardest questions are placed on the table before the implementation machinery begins to displace the substantive negotiations. 

The sequence matters. A durable settlement should first define the substantive bargain for consideration by the principals, allow them to adjust, substitute, and recalibrate its parts, and only then build the full implementation apparatus around the agreed shape. If the order is reversed, ceasefire mechanics, monitoring architecture, and sanctions schedules first; final status later, or not at all, the likely result is not peace but a managed pause. 

The war machine may slow, but the triggers remain alive, and the process may never return to the overlapping imagined communities, constitutional questions, and territorial status adjustments that must be addressed for peace to take root.

- Establish the draft architecture as a single negotiating text and procedural starting point, not as a take-it-or-leave-it packaged deal.

## **9. Why the United States Should Be Involved** 

The United States is not neutral in the ordinary sense. It may nevertheless be more feasible as lead broker and implementation partner than Europe alone. Russia is unlikely to accept Europe as the dominant actor of a settlement that defines Russia's post-war security environment. 

### **Why the United States is comparatively feasible as lead broker** 

- **Leverage.** Substantial influence over Ukrainian military support, intelligence cooperation, financing, sanctions, and external legitimacy. 

- **Distance from the theatre.** Unlike Europe, the United States is not geographically embedded in the immediate territorial-security contest. This can make a bounded broker role easier to present. 

- **Ability to coordinate with Europe.** Europe is indispensable to reconstruction, sanctions, and economic support, but need not be the public lead of the settlement. 

- **Credibility.** The United States can combine diplomatic reach, intelligence, sanctions leverage, financial weight, and convening capacity at a scale unavailable to other external actors. 

- **Initial executive and diplomatic implementation bridge** . Longer-term financial, sanctions, verification, or security commitments remain subject to United States constitutional and statutory processes, however facilitating the peace deal makes later congressional reversal or repudiation materially harder: after verified compliance and a functioning ceasefire, the burden of disrupting the settlement shifts to those who would withdraw support. 

- **Political credit.** A US president who materially brokers, facilitates, and carries a durable settlement can credibly claim to have ended a major European war.

## **10. Final Criterion** 

The architecture succeeds only if all of the following become true: 

- Ukraine survives as a sovereign, armed, independent, neutral state. 

- Russia can implement the agreement without experiencing it as regime-threatening. 

- Ukraine's new constitutional structure is ratified in a referendum, both nationally and in the eastern area. 

- Phased, symmetric force-integration arrangements must credibly preserve armed self-defence capability without producing regional secession, a de facto armed east-west partition, or externally controlled paralysis. 

- Crimea operates under a jointly adopted and locally ratified constitutional settlement: neither Ukraine nor Russia holds exclusive sovereignty or military control, while local Crimean institutions govern ordinary life. 

- Sevastopol is permanently converted from a fleet base into a civilian Free Port & Trade Zone. Following a verified, finite, non-renewable fleet-relocation transition of up to 25 years, no Russian, Ukrainian, or third-state military or maritime-security asset may be based in or operate from the city. 

- The United States help implement the structure without assuming indefinite direct military responsibility. 

- Europe participates materially without becoming the visible author of a punitive order. 

- Ukraine retains access to external arms, training, intelligence, maintenance, and economic support without converting that access into permanent foreign combat infrastructure. 

- Reciprocal constitutional and domestic legal implementation in both Ukraine and Russia is treated as a final-stage condition of the package, not as a unilateral preliminary surrender. 

- The agreement remains operational and preferable to renewed war despite distrust between the principals, leadership change, sanctions adjustment, rearmament pressure, domestic political stress, and incompatible historical narratives, relying primarily on reciprocal constitutional commitments, verification, and incentive alignment rather than personal trust. 

_The objective is not perfect justice, complete agreement, or historical reconciliation It is a political architecture in which compliance with the agreement becomes and remains more rational, more politically viable, and more survivable than renewed war. Each side must therefore actively work to ensure that the opposing regime and leadership can survive negotiation, signature, and early implementation; otherwise nobody may remain capable of carrying the agreement to operational stability._ **_This is the minimum required counterparty risk management_ .**

## **12. Conclusion and Next Steps** 

This blueprint does not claim or intend to provide a finished peace agreement. It proposes a possible settlement geometry in which Ukraine regains security, independence, and restoration of the pre-2014 Ukraine–Russia land border; Russia obtains protected Russian-language life and a neutral Ukraine that cannot serve as a foreign military platform; Crimea ceases to be subject to exclusive sovereignty or military control by either principal; and Sevastopol becomes a civilian commercial city rather than a permanent Black Sea military trigger. 

Its central proposition is that a durable settlement need not require either Ukraine or Russia to accept total defeat. It requires each to relinquish objectives that cannot be retained without continued war, while securing outcomes that both can plausibly defend as strategic success. This includes allowing both principals to redirect national resources from prolonged military confrontation towards economic development and broader international engagement. For Russia, this includes greater freedom to diversify its external economic relationships. For Ukraine, it improves the conditions for reconstruction, investment, and demographic recovery. 

The blueprint’s structure is therefore conservative in the strict sense: it preserves the most valuable viable objectives of each principal, while treating the sacrifice of lower-value, higher-risk objectives as the price of making peace achievable. 

Both sides must avoid a merely superficial reading of the Blueprint as a deeper reading is <u>indispensable for a full understanding of all it entails before deciding if it affords sufficient grounds for</u> starting talks.

## **Source Note and Scope** 

Peace plans fail when they seek to stop the present war without changing the conditions that make war rational for either party. The historical problem is not lack of peace-process failure experience. It is an inability to convert experience into causal diagnosis, and causal diagnosis into changed settlement architecture. Conflict can often be traced back to tension between different “imagined communities”, making an understanding of these a precondition for solving the underlying problem. 

This Blueprint is a conceptual framework for addressing the underlying problems. It presents option families and structural boundaries, not technical implementation manuals.
